Case study · Game design

Teaching people to identify vintage clothing by its silhouette.

Pattern Play is a web game built on real sewing patterns. Players read the silhouettes and styles of patterns from the 1930s through the 1980s and learn to tell which decade each one came from.

Piece 1 of 7

Process

I knew I wanted to teach fashion. I didn't know how.

I set out to make a game that teaches people about fashion, and then struggled with how to actually do it. Three questions kept coming up:

  • Do I teach the history of runway fashion?
  • How can I teach through customization?
  • How does this game stand out from every other fashion game?

Runway history was too big, and most fashion games already let you dress up a doll and stop there. Vintage sewing patterns answered all three. The subject is specific, it teaches the decades through the cut, style and silhouette, and customizing could come back as a reward in the Pattern Book. Prototyping in Figma started once that concept was set.

Piece 2 of 7

The core idea

No color, no print, no model. Just the cut.

Every round shows the technical flat sketch from the back of a real pattern envelope. With nothing else to go on, players have to read the silhouette, the seams and the details that actually date a garment.

The audience is specific on purpose: people who love vintage fashion, sewing and history. Getting that specific is what made the game feel like its own little world.

Piece 3 of 7

How it plays

Guess, get the story, keep the pattern.

Wrong answers aren't a dead end. The host tells you the pattern's real decade and says "We'll try this one again," and missed patterns come back for review at the end of the level. Each decade also carries a short style cue and fabric facts, like feed-sack cotton in the Depression or Lycra and the '80s aerobics craze.

Piece 5 of 7

Design system

I built everything around the illustrations.

I struggled with the visual style until I went back to why I love vintage patterns in the first place: the illustrations. So I leaned into a hand-crafted, cut-and-paste look and let every other choice support the art. The rooms are vintage illustrated interiors, the host is cut out of a pattern envelope, and the logo is a watercolor label.

Piece 6 of 7

Playtesting

One main goal, one clear flow.

Testers said the game needed a single main goal, a flow that's easy to understand, and gentler first levels. That shaped the final structure.

What I heardWhat I changed
"The game needs one main goal."Every round asks the same question: which pattern was made in this decade? Customizing moved into the Pattern Book as a reward, so it never competes with the main goal.
"The flow needs to be easy to understand."One repeating loop of guess, reveal, next. A progress bar shows how far into the level you are, and levels unlock in order.
"It needs easier levels to start."The first two levels are marked Very Easy. They show only two cards at a time and compare decades that are far apart, so the difference in silhouette is easy to spot.
